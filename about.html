How the scores work

What the gap means, the data I used and the limits.

What this is

This page comes from my dissertation at Indiana University Bloomington. It covers 367 counties in five states: California, Florida, Indiana, New York and Ohio.

For each county it compares need with four kinds of help: syringe programs, naloxone, treatment and recovery housing. The county page itself is not public yet.

What the gap means

The gap is a county's need score minus its services score, compared with the other counties in its state.

Need can't be scored

CDC doesn't report fewer than 10 overdose deaths, and some counties have no HIV rate. 37 counties, mostly small and rural.

The gap is a starting flag, not a verdict. The county page always shows the pieces behind it, especially which service is missing.

How the scores are built

Every score compares a county only with other counties in its own state, because each state collects data differently.

  1. Rank

    Rank each county on each measure, 0 (lowest in the state) to 100 (highest).

  2. Average

    Average the 3 need ranks and the 4 service ranks.

  3. Rank again

    Rank the two averages within the state, 0 to 100, as the CDC's Social Vulnerability Index does.

  4. Subtract

    Need score minus services score is the gap.

Example: LaPorte County, Indiana Need 92 − Services 3 = Gap 89

No syringe program and no recovery housing. The nearest syringe program is 84 miles away.

Every address was mapped to its county, then counted per 100,000 people.

State law is coded in six areas: syringe programs, Good Samaritan overdose protection, naloxone access, drug checking, overdose prevention centers and HIV criminalization. Each law is scored:

0bans it or says nothing 1allowed with conditions 2clearly authorized and protected

Scores add up within each area and are compared area by area, not as one total.

Limitations

  • Within-state only. Scores compare counties inside one state. They don't show which state is better or worse overall.
  • Lists differ by state. Some services had a state list with street addresses; others only a federal or national list.
  • Sites, not capacity. A program open two hours a week counts the same as one open every day.
  • Straight-line distance, within the state. Real travel takes longer, and border counties may have closer help across the state line.
  • Suppressed data. Counties with fewer than 10 overdose deaths over three years have no need score.
  • Small counties. Rates per 100,000 people can swing a lot where few people live.
  • Ties. Most counties have no syringe program, so they share the same rank on that measure.
  • Preliminary. These results are from a dissertation in progress and may change.

How the scores work

I collected the service lists, geocoded every address, coded state law and chose every measure and decision behind the scores. The analysis is written in R and Quarto, and each decision is logged in the project's decision notes.

Use of AI tools

I used AI tools (Claude) to help write and check code and to audit the data and numbers. I reviewed the results, re-checked key numbers against the data and made the final decisions myself.

No AI produces the scores. Every number comes from the data and the four steps above.

Questions about how it works are welcome.
